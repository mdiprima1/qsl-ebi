## Slide 1 — Scenario: Two savers, ten years apart

Two people each invest three hundred dollars a month in the same portfolio, earning seven point seven percent a year — the average annual return, from two thousand five to twenty twenty-six, of a classic sixty forty portfolio: sixty percent stocks and forty percent bonds. Later units show exactly how such numbers are measured. One starts at twenty-five. The other starts at thirty-five. Both keep investing until sixty-five. By then the early starter has deposited one hundred forty-four thousand dollars, and the late starter one hundred eight thousand — twenty-five percent less. Before the next slide, look at the chart and make a guess. The late starter put in twenty-five percent less money. Does he end twenty-five percent behind — or further?

## Slide 2 — Intuition: The earliest money works the longest

A natural answer runs through the deposits: the early starter put in a third more money, so he should finish about a third ahead. The chart says otherwise. At sixty-five the accounts stand at about eight hundred ninety thousand dollars against about four hundred thousand — the early starter finishes with more than twice as much. The deposits alone cannot explain that gap. What explains it is this: money that stays invested earns money, and those earnings stay invested and earn money of their own. The ten extra years belong to the earliest dollars. A dollar deposited at twenty-five has forty years to earn, and its earnings have decades to earn too. The gap came from time, not from the deposits.

## Slide 3 — Definition: Compound growth

The idea has a formal name: compound growth. An investment grows by a percentage of its current value, so each year's growth builds on all the growth that came before. One line of arithmetic describes it: the ending value equals the starting amount, multiplied by one plus the annual return, once for every year invested. One step of it: one hundred thousand dollars at seven point seven percent becomes one hundred seven thousand seven hundred after one year. The next year's seven point seven percent lands on the new, larger amount — and that is the whole mechanism.

## Slide 4 — Worked example: One hundred thousand dollars, thirty years

Let's walk the formula through thirty years. One hundred thousand dollars grows at seven point seven percent a year, with nothing added and nothing taken out. Year one earns seven thousand seven hundred. Year two starts from one hundred seven thousand seven hundred, so the same seven point seven percent earns eight thousand two hundred ninety-three. By year ten, the balance entering the year is nearly one hundred ninety-five thousand dollars, and the year earns about fifteen thousand — nearly double year one. And by year thirty, a single year earns about sixty-six thousand dollars — more than eight times year one. The rate never changed. The balance it landed on did.

## Slide 5 — Considerations: What the steady rate leaves out

Four things the steady rate leaves out. First, no market pays the same rate every year. The seven point seven percent on these slides is an average over two thousand five to twenty twenty-six; the real path swings around it, some years far above, some far below. Later units measure those swings. Second, the rate and the years multiply. Five hundred dollars a month for forty years ends at about three hundred sixty-five thousand dollars at two percent, about one point five million at seven point seven percent, and about two point seven million at nine point nine percent. The deposits are identical in every row — the return decides the size of the result. Third, these figures are before costs, taxes and inflation; each takes its share, and the course returns to all three. And fourth, the return number is the subject of this course: a difference of two percentage points, held for decades, separates outcomes by hundreds of thousands of dollars. One question to close. If the return is that decisive, what would it take to trust a number like seven point seven percent before building forty years of saving on it? That question is where the rest of the course begins.
