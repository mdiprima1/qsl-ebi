# Unit 1 — Compounding — narration, per slide (26-slide deck)

Voice: `qsl-hq/standards/VOICE-LOADOUT.md` stack. Numbers spelled for the ear; every figure traces to `data_unit1_compounding.csv` or `qsl-research/demo-strategies/overview-table.md`. Matches `unit1_deck.html` / `storyboard.md`.

**01 — Opener.** Welcome to Evidence-Based Investing for Everyone, from the Quant Strategy Lab. This first unit is about compounding: money that stays invested earns money, and those earnings earn money of their own. There is no strategy on these slides yet, and no method. One line of arithmetic, two levers — the years and the rate — and what they make possible. Every number you will see is computed, and its source is stated.

**02 — Two savers, ten years apart.** Two people each invest three hundred dollars a month in the same portfolio, earning seven point seven percent a year — the average annual return, from two thousand five to twenty twenty-six, of a classic sixty forty portfolio. One starts at twenty-five. The other starts at thirty-five. Both keep going until sixty-five. The early starter deposits one hundred forty-four thousand dollars in total; the late starter one hundred eight thousand — twenty-five percent less. Before the next slide, commit to a guess. Does the late starter end twenty-five percent behind — or further?

**03 — Less than half.** The accounts at sixty-five stand at eight hundred ninety thousand dollars against four hundred thousand. The late starter deposited twenty-five percent less — and finishes fifty-five percent behind. He ends with less than half. The deposits cannot explain that gap; something else in the account did most of the work. This unit explains what — and, near the end, what it means if your situation is the second curve.

**04 — Money earning money.** Watch one account with no deposits at all: one hundred thousand dollars at seven point seven percent. Year one earns seven thousand seven hundred. Year two starts from a larger balance, so the same rate earns eight thousand two hundred ninety-three. By year ten, the year earns about fifteen thousand. By year thirty, a single year earns about sixty-six thousand dollars — more than eight times year one. The rate never changed. The balance it lands on did.

**05 — After forty years.** Run the account to year forty and it stands at about one point nine five million dollars — nineteen times where it began, with nothing added along the way. The account did not grow by the same dollars each year. It grew by the same proportion of an ever-larger base. That distinction is the whole mechanism.

**06 — Compound growth.** The mechanism has a formal name: compound growth. The ending value equals the starting amount, multiplied by one plus the annual return, once for every year. P is the starting amount; r is the return as a decimal — seven point seven percent is zero point zero seven seven; n counts the years. One step: one hundred thousand times one point zero seven seven is one hundred seven thousand seven hundred — and the next year's growth lands on that new, larger amount.

**07 — The formula's shape.** Three things the formula's shape tells you. The rate and the years do not add — they compound; doubling either does far more than double the ending once the years are many. The formula has no memory of effort — it asks only how much, at what rate, for how long. And it runs backward: given the ending you need and the years you have, it names the rate that gets you there. We use that direction near the end of this unit.

**08 — Lever one: the rate.** Save five hundred dollars a month for forty years — two hundred forty thousand deposited in every case — and change only the return. At two percent, a savings account, the deposits become three hundred sixty-five thousand dollars. At seven point seven percent, one and a half million. At nine point nine percent, the S and P five hundred's average over the same period, two point seven million. The return alone separates the endings by a factor of more than seven.

**09 — Lever two: the years.** The cost of delay is heaviest at the front, because the earliest deposits compound the longest. Start at twenty-six instead of twenty-five — one year of waiting, three thousand six hundred dollars not yet deposited — and the ending value at sixty-five drops by about sixty-seven thousand dollars. The first year's deposits are the most valuable dollars in the whole plan.

**10 — Catching up with money alone.** Suppose the thirty-five-starter wants to reach the twenty-five-starter's eight hundred ninety thousand at sixty-five, at the same seven point seven percent. The required saving is about six hundred seventy dollars a month, for thirty years — more than double, every month. That is what catching up costs when money is the only lever you pull. But money is not the only lever.

**11 — Maya.** Three lives, one formula. Maya is twenty-two and starts from zero: three hundred dollars a month until sixty-five. At two percent she reaches two hundred forty-five thousand dollars, which then pays about eight hundred a month to age ninety-five. At seven point seven percent: one point one five million, paying about seven thousand six hundred fifty a month. At nine point nine percent: two point one five million — about eighteen thousand a month. The saving is identical in the three rows; the retirement it buys is not.

**12 — Daniel.** Daniel is forty, with fifty thousand saved and one thousand a month to invest. He wants to stop working at sixty — twenty years away. At two percent he reaches three hundred seventy thousand, paying about one thousand one hundred a month. At seven point seven percent: seven hundred seventy thousand and five thousand one hundred a month. At nine point nine percent: one point zero five million and eight thousand four hundred fifty. Whether Daniel can stop at sixty is as much a return question as a savings question.

**13 — Rosa.** Rosa is sixty-five and retiring now with one million dollars. Her question runs the formula backward: what can the million pay each month to ninety-five, keeping a fifth in reserve? At two percent, about three thousand four hundred. At seven point seven percent, about seven thousand four hundred. At nine point nine percent, about nine thousand one hundred fifty. The saving is finished; the return is not. During thirty years of payouts, the money still in the account keeps compounding — and the rate decides how fast the pot refills as it drains.

**14 — What the three share.** Nobody's outcome was decided by willpower alone. In every row, the deposits were held fixed and the return moved the result by multiples. That is not an argument against saving — without Maya's three hundred a month there is nothing to compound. It is an argument that the return you accept is a decision with consequences as large as the saving itself, and it deserves the same seriousness.

**15 — Why the eye underestimates growth.** Think back to your guess on slide two. If it came in low, you are in well-documented company. People misjudge compound growth in a predictable direction: we extend curves as if they were straight lines. The research literature calls it exponential growth bias, and in household survey data, more-biased households save less, borrow more, and prefer shorter horizons. They underweight precisely the quantity this unit is about.

**16 — Calculate rather than guess.** Two conclusions follow. First, do not trust intuition on compounding questions — trust the formula, which replaces a guess that is reliably wrong with a calculation that is exactly right. Second, expect the bias to invite two mistakes: starting later than you should, and treating small differences in the rate as unimportant. The bias makes both feel safe. The arithmetic says neither is.

**17 — A one percent fee, forty years.** The same mechanism runs against you, and a common place to meet it is fees. One hundred thousand dollars at seven point seven percent for forty years reaches about one point nine five million. The same account at seven point seven percent minus a one percent annual fee reaches about one point three five million. The fee consumed roughly thirty-one percent of the ending value. Not one percent — thirty-one.

**18 — Why small drags cost multiples.** The fee does not just take one percent of each year's balance; it removes money that would have compounded for every remaining year. Early fee dollars are exactly like early deposit dollars — the most powerful in the account — and the fee takes them first. Judge any yearly cost the way you judge a yearly return: one percent against an expected seven point seven is more than a tenth of it, every year. The same logic covers fund expenses, trading costs, idle cash, and debt, where compounding runs against the borrower. And all figures in this unit are before taxes and inflation — both take their share; they change the sizes, not the mechanism.

**19 — The menu of real rates.** The rates in this unit are not invented, and they are not the whole menu. Five measured average annual returns, each from a QSL backtest, each over a stated window. The Defender: six point two percent — money doubling about every twelve years. The classic sixty forty portfolio: seven point seven. The S and P five hundred over its full history: seven point nine. The Diversifier: fourteen point one — doubling about every five years. And The Growth Engine: forty-one point eight percent — doubling about every twenty months. Unit three shows exactly how numbers like these are measured.

**20 — Reading the menu.** The span is the point: real, rules-based approaches have delivered anywhere from about six to about forty percent a year over their measured windows. This is a first, deliberately partial view — the named strategies travel with you through this course, and their full stories come later. Two readings to carry. Windows matter: the higher rows were measured over twenty fifteen to twenty twenty-six, an unusually strong period, and noticing that is a skill this course teaches. And one hint, ahead of its lesson: a strategy that compounds faster asks more of the investor who holds it.

**21 — Trading rate for years.** Now put the menu against the two-savers problem. Catching up, priced in rate instead of money: still three hundred dollars a month, still aiming at eight hundred ninety thousand at sixty-five. Starting at twenty-five, seven point seven percent is enough — the conservative end of the menu. Starting at thirty-five, the required rate is about twelve percent. At forty, about fifteen and a half. At forty-five, about twenty-two. The formula does not close the door on a late start. It names the price.

**22 — The required rates are on the menu.** Read those required rates against the menu. Twelve percent sits below what The Diversifier delivered over its window. Fifteen and a half is the neighborhood it occupied. Twenty-two is well inside the span up to The Growth Engine. None of them is outside what measured, systematic strategies have produced. The higher rows are not picked off a shelf — they make demands, on method, on discipline, on what you can hold through — and managing those demands is a skill. It is the skill this course exists to teach. So if you are catching up, your plan depends more on the rate than an early starter's does — which means the work of choosing that rate carefully is worth more to you, not less. You are in the right place.

**23 — The rule of 72.** One mental tool makes compound thinking usable without a calculator. Divide seventy-two by the annual return in percent, and you get, close enough, the years it takes money to double. At seven point seven percent: about nine and a half years — the exact answer is nine point three. Doubling counts are how compounding becomes intuitive: forty years at seven point seven percent is about four doublings — one, two, four, eight, sixteen — nineteen times your money, because the doublings land on each other. When someone quotes you a return, convert it into a doubling time and count doublings across your horizon.

**24 — Run your own numbers.** Five minutes with your own numbers teaches more than any table of Maya's. The simulator runs the exact calculation used throughout this unit — your amounts, your ages, and six return rates from our research, each explained where you see it. Run three cases: your actual situation; your situation had you started five years earlier; and your situation with the return one percentage point lower. The first tells you where you stand. The second and third teach this unit's two lessons at the only scale that matters — yours.

**25 — The unit's questions.** Time for the unit's questions. They check what this unit set out to build: computing an ending value, pricing a delay, pricing a fee, estimating a doubling time, and running the formula backward from a pot to a payout.

**26 — Two levers, both real.** What to keep. Money that stays invested earns on its own earnings, and one line of arithmetic computes the result exactly. If you are early in your investing life, time is your lever, and the conservative end of the menu is enough to build a serious outcome. If you are starting later, the same arithmetic that priced your delay also prices your path: a higher compounding rate closes the gap, and the menu shows such rates have been delivered by real, disciplined methods. Choosing that rate well is a skill — and teaching it is exactly what the next twenty-six units do. Whether your lever is time or rate, you are in the right place to pull it honestly.
