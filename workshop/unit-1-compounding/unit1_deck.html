SLIDE 06 — DEFINITION
QSL
Compounding

Compound growth

  • Compound growth is growth on growth: an investment grows by a percentage of its current value, so each year’s growth builds on all the growth that came before.
  • Read the formula as a sentence: the starting amount, grown by the rate, once for every year.
  • One step of it: $100,000 × 1.077 = $107,700. The next year’s 7.7% lands on $107,700, not on $100,000.
FV = P × (1 + r)n
P — the starting amount invested
r — the annual return, as a decimal: 7.7% is 0.077
n — the number of years the money stays invested
FV — the ending value after those years

SLIDE 07 — THE FORMULA'S SHAPE
QSL
Compounding

What the formula's shape tells you

  • The rate and the years do not add — they compound. Doubling either one does far more than double the ending value once the years are many.
  • The formula has no memory of effort. It does not ask how hard the money was to save. It asks three questions: how much, at what rate, for how long.
  • It runs backward too. Given the ending value you need and the years you have, it tells you the rate that gets you there — this unit uses that direction near its end.

SLIDE 14 — WHAT THE THREE SHARE
QSL
Three lives, one formula

What the three cases share

  • Nobody’s outcome was decided by willpower alone. In every row the deposits were held fixed, and the return moved the result by multiples.
  • That is not an argument against saving. Without Maya’s $300 a month there is nothing to compound.
  • It is an argument about the return. The return you accept is a decision with consequences as large as the saving itself — and it deserves the same seriousness.

SLIDE 15 — WHY THE EYE UNDERESTIMATES
QSL
Compounding

Why the eye underestimates growth

  • Think back to your guess on slide two. If it came in low, you are in well-documented company.
  • People misjudge compound growth in a predictable direction: we extend curves as if they were straight lines.
  • The research literature calls it exponential growth bias. In household survey data, more-biased households save less, borrow more, and prefer shorter horizons.
  • They underweight precisely the quantity this unit is about.

SLIDE 16 — CALCULATE, DON'T GUESS
QSL
Compounding

Calculate rather than guess

  • Do not trust intuition on compounding questions — trust the formula. FV = P × (1 + r)n replaces a guess that is reliably wrong with a calculation that is exactly right.
  • Expect the bias to invite two mistakes: starting later than you should, and treating small differences in the rate as unimportant.
  • The bias makes both feel safe. The arithmetic says neither is.

SLIDE 18 — WHY SMALL DRAGS COST MULTIPLES
QSL
Compounding in reverse

Why small drags cost multiples

  • The fee removes money that would have compounded for every remaining year. Early fee dollars are exactly like early deposit dollars — the most powerful in the account — and the fee takes them first.
  • Judge any yearly cost the way you judge a yearly return. A 1% fee against an expected 7.7% return is more than a tenth of it, every year.
  • The same logic covers fund expenses, trading costs, idle cash — and debt, where compounding runs against the borrower. Later units return to it.
  • All figures in this unit are before taxes and inflation. Both take their share; they change the sizes, not the mechanism.

SLIDE 23 — THE RULE OF 72
QSL
A shortcut worth keeping

The rule of 72

  • Divide 72 by the annual return in percent: that is, close enough, the years it takes money to double.
  • Doubling counts make compounding intuitive. Forty years at 7.7% is about four doublings: 1 → 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 — landing on each other, nineteen times your money.
  • When someone quotes a return, convert it to a doubling time and count doublings across your horizon.
Four doublings at 7.7% — about 9.4 years each
